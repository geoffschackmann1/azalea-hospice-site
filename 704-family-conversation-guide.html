A guide for families · The hardest first conversation

"I think it might be time to talk about hospice."

Almost every family we meet had the same fear before they called us: that even saying the word would feel like a betrayal — a giving-up, a finality, a thing they were not allowed to choose for someone they love. This is a small guide for that conversation. There is no script that fits every family. There are only some sentences that have, in our experience, helped.

Families considering hospice

1Before the conversation

Pick a quiet hour, not a holiday, not after a hospital discharge. Sit at eye level. Have water. Have nothing to do after. The single most useful preparation is to ask yourself one question: am I trying to convince them, or am I trying to understand what they want? The conversations that go well are the second kind.

Scene 1 — Opening with a parent

You are sitting on the porch with your father. The doctor has, for the third time, used the phrase "if things don't change."

You
Dad — I want to ask you something that's been on my mind. When you think about how the next few months go, what matters most to you? Open. Not "are you ready to die." Not "the doctor says." A question only he can answer.
Father
I just want to be home. I don't want any more hospitals.
You
Okay. That's what I'd guessed. There's a kind of care that comes to the house — a nurse, an aide, somebody you can call at three in the morning — that is built around exactly that. It's called hospice. Have you thought about it? Naming the word once, plainly, then handing it back. No pitch.

Scene 2 — A sibling who is not ready

Your brother thinks calling hospice is "giving up on Mom."

Brother
I'm not signing anything that says we're done fighting.
You
I hear you. I'm not asking you to. Hospice isn't a paper that says we're done — Mom can stop it any day she wants. It's a team that comes to the house so she's not in the ER every two weeks. That's all I'm asking us to consider. Disarm the metaphor — "fight" — without arguing with it. Reframe as practical, not philosophical.
Brother
What if she gets better?
You
Then she comes off. People do — they call it graduating. It happens. The point is to have help in the house, not to predict the future.

Scene 3 — The patient who says "no"

Your mother has heard the word "hospice" and shaken her head.

Mother
I'm not ready for hospice. I'm not dying tomorrow.
You
You're not. And nobody's saying you are. Can I tell you what I learned about it that surprised me? Most families wish they'd called six months earlier than they did — because of the help, not because of the dying. A nurse in the house. Somebody to call at night. Things you've been doing alone. Would it be okay if a nurse just came and talked with us once — no commitment? Don't argue with "no." Offer a smaller yes — one visit, one conversation.

Scene 4 — When it's already late

Your spouse has declined quickly. You wonder if it's "too late."

You
I think I waited too long.
Nurse
You're here now. That counts. We can be at your house in two hours and have a hospital bed there by tonight. Whatever time is left, we make it as comfortable as we can. There is no door that closes — we just walk through whichever one is open.

2Sentences that often help

  • "What matters most to you about how this goes?" — almost always a better opening than a piece of information.
  • "Hospice is care, not a place." — the single most common misunderstanding.
  • "You can stop it any time." — true, and reassuring to people who fear a one-way door.
  • "Most families say they wished they'd called sooner." — gentle truth, no pressure.
  • "Would it be okay if a nurse just came once?" — the smaller-yes.

3Sentences to avoid

  • "There's nothing more we can do." There is always more we can do — it just isn't curative.
  • "You only have X months left." Numbers feel like sentences. Use ranges, or none.
  • "It's time to give up." It is never time to give up. It may be time to change the goal.
  • "Don't you want to be comfortable?" Of course they do. The question is leading and faintly accusatory.

"The word 'hospice' is not a wall. It is a door. The hardest part is opening it once. After that, it's almost always a relief."
