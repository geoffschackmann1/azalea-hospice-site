Brand · Voice · Editorial standards

How we sound.

Hospice copy is mostly read by people in pain. They came looking for a person, not a paragraph. The aim of every word we publish is to feel — to the reader — like a quiet voice from someone who has been here before, knows the way, and is unhurried.

1Voice — the four notes

Plainspoken, not clinical

Use the words a friend would use. "Help with breathing," not "respiratory comfort intervention." Save the clinical register for the chart, not the brochure.

Warm, not sentimental

Hospice deserves real feeling, not florid feeling. Avoid sunsets, butterflies, hands reaching toward light. Specificity is warmer than metaphor.

Calm, not somber

We are not a funeral home. Read aloud — does it sound like a hush, or like a friend talking quietly? Aim for the second.

Honest, not euphemistic

Say "die" and "death." Sparingly, kindly, but say them. The most consoling thing we can do is name what is happening.

2Do · Don't

Do

"When breathing gets harder, we'll be there."

"Mom died at home, the way she wanted."

"Hospice care is mostly time, attention, and quiet."

"Call us before you think you need to. Most families wish they had."

Don't

"Empowering end-of-life journeys with compassion."

"Mom passed away peacefully surrounded by loved ones."

"Holistic, patient-centered, family-focused care continuum."

"It's never too late — call today and we'll guide you home."

3Words we avoid

  • "Battle," "fight," "lose." Death is not a defeat. Hospice is not surrender.
  • "Passed away," "passed." Use "died" — kinder, clearer, less rehearsed.
  • "Journey," "transition." A person dying is not on a journey to anywhere; they are still here, with us.
  • "Quality of life" as a slogan. Use it only when it is specific. Otherwise it is empty.
  • Trademarked clinical jargon ("CCM™ care," "ComfortPath™") in patient-facing copy.
  • Acronyms without expansion. The first time IDG, GIP, NHPCO appears, write it out.

4House style

  • Names. First reference: "Azalea Hospice & Palliative Care." Subsequent: "Azalea Hospice" or "Azalea." Never "Avant Hospice" in patient-facing copy — that is the legal entity, used only on contracts and government filings.
  • The serial comma. Yes — pain, dignity, and quiet.
  • Numerals. Spell out one through nine; figures for 10+. Always figures for ages, dollars, percentages, and time.
  • Capitalization. Sentence case for headlines. Lowercase "hospice" except as part of a proper name.
  • Em dashes. Used sparingly, with spaces — like this — for parenthetical asides.
  • Pronouns. "We," not "Azalea Hospice professionals." "You," not "the patient" — when speaking to a patient or family.
  • One space after a period, always.

5Stories & testimonials

  • Always written or quoted with consent of the family. Photo consent is separate from quote consent.
  • Names — first names only; full name only with explicit written consent.
  • No PHI, ever — no diagnosis, location of care, or facility name without consent.
  • Hold a story for at least 90 days after a death before publishing, unless the family requests otherwise.
  • Children depicted in photos require a separate consent from the parent or guardian and, where age-appropriate, the child.

6Reading-level & accessibility

Patient-facing copy targets a 6th-grade reading level (Hemingway grade 6 or below; Flesch reading ease ≥ 70). Sentences ≤ 20 words on average. Avoid medical idiom. Provide Spanish translation for all standard family materials. Provide large-print versions of consent forms and Patient Rights on request.

7Photography direction

  • Real patients, real families, real staff. No stock imagery in patient-facing brand work.
  • Available light over flash. Quiet domestic settings over clinical settings.
  • Hands, the back of a head, a gesture, a room — these often carry more than a posed portrait.
  • Smiles are welcome but not required. Honesty over cheer.

"Write the way the nurse speaks at three in the morning when the family is afraid. That is our voice."
