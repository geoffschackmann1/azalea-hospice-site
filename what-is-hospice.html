What is hospice?

The plain answer. Not a place — a team.

Hospice is a type of medical care for people who have a serious illness and are no longer seeking a cure. The goal is not to make someone live longer or to shorten their life. The goal is to make the time that remains as comfortable, meaningful, and peaceful as possible.

It is covered by Medicare, Medicaid, and most private insurance. The person receiving care usually pays nothing.

Hospice is not a place. Most hospice care happens wherever you live — at home, in an assisted living facility, or in a nursing home. A team of doctors, nurses, aides, chaplains, and social workers comes to you.

What hospice provides

When someone chooses hospice, they get:

  • A nurse who visits regularly and is on call 24 hours a day, 7 days a week
  • A doctor who oversees the plan of care, working with the patient's own doctor
  • Medication, equipment, and supplies related to the hospice diagnosis — hospital beds, oxygen, walkers, pain medication, and more
  • A home health aide to help with bathing, dressing, and personal care
  • A chaplain for spiritual care, whatever the patient's faith tradition
  • A social worker to help with advance directives, caregiver resources, and emotional support
  • Bereavement support for the family for 13 months after the death — free, whether or not the death occurred on hospice service

Who pays for it

Hospice is one of the most comprehensive benefits in American healthcare. If you have:

  • Medicare Part A — the hospice benefit covers everything. No copay, no deductible
  • Medicaid — covers hospice in Texas with the same comprehensive benefit as Medicare
  • Private insurance — most plans include a hospice benefit. We verify your coverage at no cost

What it costs the patient: $0. Hospice is the only Medicare benefit that covers not just medical care, but medication, equipment, supplies, and emotional and spiritual support — all at no cost to the patient.

What stays the same

Choosing hospice does not mean giving up control. The patient still:

  • Keeps their own doctor (the hospice medical director works with them)
  • Makes their own decisions about care
  • Can stop hospice at any time, for any reason
  • Can still receive treatment for conditions unrelated to the hospice diagnosis

Common misconceptions

"Hospice means giving up hope."

Hope changes shape, but does not disappear. Many families find that hospice brings a different kind of hope — the hope of comfort, of peace, of being at home, of saying what needs to be said. The hope that a loved one will not die in pain or alone.

"Hospice is only for the last few days."

Hospice is intended for people with a life expectancy of six months or less, but many patients receive care for months. The earlier someone starts hospice, the more benefit they and their family receive from the full range of services.

"Hospice means leaving home."

Over 95% of hospice care is delivered in the patient's home or wherever they live. The team comes to you.

"Hospice speeds up death."

Hospice does not hasten or postpone death. The focus is on managing symptoms and improving quality of life. Some patients actually live longer on hospice than they would with aggressive treatment, because the body is not under the stress of invasive procedures.

"I can't have hospice and still see my doctor."

Your primary doctor remains involved. The hospice team works alongside them, sharing information and coordinating care.
