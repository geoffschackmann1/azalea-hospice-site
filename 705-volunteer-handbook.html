For applicants & new volunteers

Volunteers are the difference between hospice and just home health.

Federal regulation requires that volunteers provide at least 5% of total patient-care hours. We exceed it because volunteers are the part of our team with the rarest gift: unhurried time.

1What a hospice volunteer does — and doesn't

You do bring your presence. Sit. Listen. Read aloud. Watch a movie with someone whose family needs an hour to grocery-shop. Hold a hand. Ask what someone wants for their birthday. Let silence be okay.

You don't give medications, transfer patients, change wound dressings, drive in your own car without authorization, or — and this is hardest — fix things. The most common mistake new volunteers make is treating the visit like a problem to solve. It isn't. It is company.

2Roles you can choose

Companionship visits

The classic role. Weekly visits to a patient's home, AL/SNF room, or hospice unit. 1–3 hours. Conversation, reading, music, presence.

Caregiver respite

Sit with the patient while the family caregiver goes to the grocery store, the doctor, or out to dinner. The single most-asked-for help.

Vigil volunteer (11th-hour)

Trained to sit with patients in the final hours when family cannot be present. On-call rotation. Demanding but the most meaningful work many volunteers do.

Veteran-to-veteran

Veterans visiting Veteran patients. Pinning ceremonies. Story-listening. We are a "We Honor Veterans" Level 4 partner.

Pet therapy

You and a certified therapy dog or cat. Visits to homes and the hospice unit. Animals must hold current certification through TDI, Pet Partners, or equivalent.

Bereavement support

Phone calls, condolence cards, support-group co-facilitation, anniversary check-ins. Often a path for volunteers who have themselves been bereaved.

Office & administrative

Mailings, data entry, supply organization. Lower-touch but vital. Counts toward the federal volunteer-hour requirement.

Memory work

Helping patients record life stories, write letters to grandchildren, assemble photo books. A growing program.

3How you become a volunteer

  1. Application & interview. 30-minute conversation with the Volunteer Coordinator. We ask about your motivation — including any recent losses. We may ask you to wait if a loss is less than 12 months old; it is for your protection, not a rejection.
  2. Background check, TB screen, references. Required by Texas HHSC and Medicare CoP §418.78.
  3. Orientation & training. 16 hours, see schedule below.
  4. Shadow visits. Two visits with an experienced volunteer or social worker before solo assignment.
  5. Match. Coordinator pairs you with a patient based on geography, schedule, interests, and IDG recommendation.
  6. Ongoing. Monthly volunteer support meetings; documentation after every visit; coordinator check-in every 90 days.

5Boundaries we ask you to hold

  • No money. Do not lend, accept, or carry money for a patient or family. Ever.
  • No personal transportation. Do not drive a patient in your car unless authorized in writing for that visit.
  • No medications. Do not handle or administer any medication, including over-the-counter.
  • No medical advice. "I'll ask the nurse" is the right answer to almost every clinical question.
  • No outside contact after death. Bereavement contact is coordinated by our team, not by individual volunteers, for the first 90 days.
  • Gifts of nominal value (under $25) may be accepted; document them. Anything more, decline kindly and tell the coordinator.
